# TripleDart + Slate: Growth-Round Pitch Deck Audit

**Deck reviewed:** "TripleDart + Slate: The Palantir for B2B Growth" (October 2026, 31 slides)
**Cross-checked against:** `TripleDart_Slate_Investor_Deck.pdf` (the August 2026, 15-slide version in this repo)
**Round:** $15M ($12M primary, $3M secondary). Strategic growth partner to help reach $100M
**Target investors:** Bay Area, New York, London, Dubai and Bangalore growth funds; large family offices; corporate venture arms (Sony, Panasonic, Huawei)
**Date of audit:** 22 October 2026

---

## 0. The verdict in one page

**The business is fundable. This deck isn't ready to send yet.**

The underlying company is rare. It has a $6.5M run-rate, about 50% EBITDA, no outside capital, $0 spent on paid acquisition, and enterprise logos (GE Appliances, Cognizant, Payoneer, MakeMyTrip) that most AI-native services startups don't have. The writing is crisp, the design is premium, and the 2027 plan has a funnel and an owner behind each number. That last part puts it ahead of most decks at this stage.

But a growth-stage partner underwrites **evidence of compounding**, not a category claim. On that test the deck has four gaps that would end most first meetings:

1. **Every chart in the deck is a forecast. No chart shows the past.** There's no revenue history, no growth rate, and no quarterly trend. You're asking for a 131% jump in 2027 ($6.5M → $15M) without showing what the last three years looked like.
2. **Retention is the elephant, and it's buried.** About 64% NRR and about 4% monthly gross churn (roughly 39% of revenue lost per year) appear only as "before" numbers on slide 20. Any growth investor will find this in the first hour of diligence. If they find it before you explain it, you lose control of the story.
3. **The headline promise ("priced on outcomes") is a 2027 goal, not today's model.** Slide 13 says "$5–30K a month per lane, on retainer", and slide 22 makes "outcome pricing standard" a Q3 2027 milestone. The title, mission and closing slide all present it as current.
4. **The valuation logic leans on software (Sierra, Decagon, Palantir), but software is 8% of revenue** and the deck has no product evidence: no screenshots, no usage data, no automation metrics.

There are also **internal-draft artifacts on at least 12 slides** ([verify], [x]/[y] placeholders, "confirm with the account team", bracketed multiples, [email]/[phone]) and **at least 8 arithmetic inconsistencies** (section 3). A diligence analyst will spot both in minutes.

**My thesis for the rewrite:** you're asking to be priced like an AI-native platform while your P&L reads like a very good services firm. The deck has to *prove the transition is happening* with trend data: revenue per delivery FTE going up, cost-to-serve going down, Slate growing outside TripleDart accounts, and churn falling. Without that, investors default to services multiples and the conversation is about price, not partnership. With it, the 50% EBITDA becomes the most powerful slide you have, because almost no AI-native company can show it.

**Recommended timing:** use November and December for relationship meetings with a short teaser. Run the formal process in **January and February 2027 with Q4 2026 actuals**, including the first quarter of falling churn and Slate growth. One quarter of proof that churn is moving from 4% toward 2.5% is worth more than any slide.

## 1. What excites me (keep these and make them louder)

1. **Profitability at this scale with no outside capital.** About 50% EBITDA on roughly $6.5M is about $3.25M a year of cash generation. It changes your negotiating position: you're raising to go faster, not to survive. Lead with it, but show it on a full fiscal year and not only June–September (see section 5).
2. **$0 on paid marketing or sales commission.** That implies referral- and reputation-led demand, which is excellent capital efficiency. It's also a question (section 6, G3), so prepare the answer.
3. **Slide 2, "Above $5K a month, nobody buys a tool. They hire an owner."** This is the best insight in the deck. It explains why services and software can sit in one company, and it segments the market by willingness to pay. It should be slide 3 in the new structure.
4. **Enterprise logos sold from an India-delivered model.** GE Appliances and Cognizant are proof that a global enterprise will run US performance through your team. For the Japanese corporate venture arms in particular, "a global conglomerate's US unit trusts us with its pipeline" is the most relevant proof point you have (GE Appliances is owned by Haier).
5. **The 2027 plan is an operating plan, not a wish.** Funnels with conversion rates (12.5 meetings a win in the US, 8.6 in India and RoW), quarterly checkpoints, a named owner per number, and a monthly investor scorecard. Growth partners love this, because it shows how you'll behave after the money lands.
6. **The TripleDart 2027 math ties out.** $6.0M × 85% NRR = $5.1M retained, plus 92 new clients × $76K = $7.0M, which gives about $12.1M against the $12M target. That internal consistency is credible. Say so in the appendix.
7. **"What must be true" (slide 27).** Writing your own bear-case conditions is a mature move. Keep it.
8. **Timing.** "Services as software" and AI roll-ups (the General Catalyst and Thrive pattern on slide 26) is one of the most active growth theses of 2025–26. GEO and AI search is a new budget line that buyers are actively trying to staff.
9. **Slate momentum, if it holds.** The August deck said $30K MRR. October says about $0.5M run-rate (about $40–42K MRR). That's roughly 35–40% growth in two months. Show it as a monthly chart.

## 2. What doesn't excite me (the gaps a growth investor will find)

### 2.1 No history, so the forecast can't be judged
A growth investor's first move is to put your plan next to your trailing growth. The deck has no FY23–FY26 revenue, no quarterly run-rate trend, and no growth rate. Without it, a 131% growth year in 2027, and a Q1 2027 that adds only $0.9M before the curve steepens, reads as a hockey stick. Of the year's $8.5M of net new ARR, 68% lands in Q3 and Q4.

**Fix:** add a "five years, bootstrapped" chart (annual revenue plus EBITDA) and an 8-quarter run-rate chart split into TripleDart and Slate. If trailing growth is, say, 30%, then your Rule of 40 score is about 80 (30% growth plus 50% EBITDA). That's a headline number in its own right, so put it on the cover.

### 2.2 Retention is weak and unexplained
- About 4% monthly gross churn compounds to roughly **39% of revenue lost a year**. That's the main reason investors will hesitate to call this "ARR".
- About 64% NRR today. The deck gives three different 2027 NRR numbers: a 75% "floor" (slide 30), 85% for the year (slide 20), and a "100% run-rate" by Q4 (slide 22).
- The plan takes churn from 4% to 2.5% (Q2) to 1.5% (Q3) **within nine months**. For a services book that's a big operational claim, and it carries the whole plan.

**Fix:** a dedicated "revenue quality" slide that:
- splits retention by segment (enterprise is very likely much stickier than the 66 "core" clients) and by lane (SEO vs paid vs RevOps),
- shows quarterly cohort revenue retention,
- separates pilot churn (planned 90-day "lands" that don't convert) from retainer churn. Your land-on-a-90-day-target model mechanically inflates churn, so say so,
- shows the leading indicators already moving (health score, triggers, AM coverage) and the Q4 2026 trend.

### 2.3 "Priced on outcomes" isn't today's reality
The title slide, mission, slide 17 and closing slide all say you're outcome-priced. Slide 13 says retainer, and slide 22 says outcome pricing becomes standard in Q3 2027. An investor who reads closely will see this as overclaiming, and it will taint everything else.

**Fix:** say exactly what's true. "Every contract carries a written pipeline target, reported weekly from the client's CRM. If we're behind plan, we add work at no charge. Variable, outcome-linked fees become standard in 2027 (X% of new contracts already have them)." That's still a strong differentiator. It's also honest, and growth investors notice the difference.

### 2.4 The software story has no software in it
Palantir, Sierra and Decagon are all product companies with very high gross margins. Your deck shows no product, no screenshots, no agent workflow, no usage data and no automation percentage. The August deck at least had a Slate product slide ("Watches → Plans → Executes → Learns"). It's been dropped.

**Fix:** one product slide with real screenshots, plus a 60–90 second demo link or QR code. One "AI leverage" slide with trend lines:
- revenue per delivery FTE by quarter (about $81K per head today on ~80 people; show it rising),
- delivery hours per $1K of revenue (falling),
- % of recurring tasks executed by Slate agents,
- number of playbooks shipped into Slate (the "custom solve becomes a playbook within 30 days" claim needs a count),
- Slate revenue from **non-TripleDart** customers vs TripleDart-attached.

### 2.5 Two companies, one CEO: the structure is unclear
"One CEO, two companies, seven lanes" raises immediate questions the deck doesn't answer. Which entity is the investor buying into? Is there a holdco? Where is it domiciled (India, US, Singapore)? Do TripleDart and Slate have the same cap table? Does Slate charge TripleDart clients separately, or is Slate revenue partly an internal allocation?

**Fix:** a one-line structure note on the ask slide, and a full structure and cap-table page in the appendix. Decide the holdco question before going out (section 7).

### 2.6 The team slide undersells the founders
Slide 23 shows "Gayu", "Sabari", "Manoj", "JK", "SN", "Mahesh", "Joe". Slide 9 says "4 co-founders" but doesn't name them. Slide 9's footer says "Company names to confirm with each person before sending." For a growth partner, the team *is* the bet on getting from $15M to $100M.

**Fix:** full names, photos, one line of prior roles and outcomes, years at the company, and the lane each person owns. Add the planned advisory board names if you have any (one strong US CMO name is worth a slide). Bring back two numbers from the August deck: **<10% attrition over 4 years** and the 2,000+ articles a year delivery scale.

### 2.7 The market sizing is top-down and inconsistent
$450B (global agency market) and $4.6T (services AI turns into software) are other people's numbers, and both include B2C. Neither is your market. The August deck said **$1.5B TAM and $100–280M SAM**. An investor who saw both will ask which one is true.

**Fix:** one bottoms-up number. Count of B2B companies with $10M–$1B revenue in US, UK, EU, India, GCC and Japan × the share that buy outside growth help × annual wallet ($60–360K). Then show the $450B only as context ("the pool AI will reprice").

### 2.8 The ask contradicts itself
- Slide 28: "Our profits fund the 2027 organisation build. The round funds the agents, the US engine and acquisitions." And the headline: "$15M to build the next curve: from $15M of ARR to $100M."
- Slide 29: "Every dollar of the $12M… Total primary → **$15M exit ARR in 2027**."
- Slide 25: "The 2027 org build costs about $1.6M a year… [refresh in the AOP]."

So does the round fund getting to $15M, or getting from $15M to $100M? Investors will also ask: "You generate about $3M a year. Why do you need $12M?" (section 6, L1).

**Fix:** one consistent story. "Profits fund the base plan to ~$10–11M (the August deck's standalone case). The round buys the extra $4–5M in 2027 (US engine plus Slate) and the platform for M&A in 2028+." Then show both cases side by side. That's the clearest way to justify primary capital in a profitable company.

### 2.9 Acquisitions are in the story but not really in the budget
"One or two acquisitions a year, run on Slate" is in the plan and in "what must be true", but the budget is **$1.0M**. That buys perhaps one very small agency. Services growth of 70–75% a year organically from $12M to $60M (2027 → 2030) is unusual, so the $100M path almost certainly depends on M&A.

**Fix:** split the $100M path into organic and inorganic. Add an M&A slide (criteria, pipeline count, integration playbook, a target multiple like 0.5–1.5× revenue for agencies, and earn-out structure). Either raise the M&A allocation or say a follow-on and debt facility funds it.

### 2.10 The $1B math will be challenged
Services at 4–6× revenue sits near the top of what listed IT services trade at, and agencies trade far lower (holding companies at roughly 0.5–1.5× revenue). With ~4% monthly churn, investors will put you in the agency bucket, not the IT-services bucket. The Sierra comparison ($15.8B on ~$150M ARR, over 100×) invites eye-rolls when 92% of your revenue is services. Notably, the August deck valued services on **EBIT multiples**, which is more credible.

**Fix:** sum-of-the-parts with services on EBITDA and Slate on ARR. Remove the Sierra valuation sentence. Keep Sierra and Decagon only as *delivery-model* analogies on the "pattern" slide.

### 2.11 The risk slide misses the risks investors actually worry about
Slide 30 lists operational risks (churn, hiring, self-serve, sellers, founder bandwidth). It misses the strategic ones:
- **AI commoditization.** Clients could build in-house with agents. Google, OpenAI, HubSpot and others could bundle GEO.
- **Outcome-contract liability.** What does "we add work at no charge" cost when you miss? Attribution disputes.
- **Data rights.** Your moat ("capture outcome data → train better agents") requires the right to use client data. Enterprise MSAs (GE, Cognizant) usually prohibit this.
- **Concentration** (customer, channel, Google Ads dependency) and **key-person** risk (one CEO across two companies).
- **FX and regulation** (INR/USD, DPDP Act, GDPR, transfer pricing).

### 2.12 The Palantir headline is a risk with *this* investor list
- It invites a comparison you lose on day one (Palantir is about 80% gross-margin software; you're 8% software).
- Palantir is a US defense and intelligence brand. It reads differently in Tokyo, Dubai, and especially to a Huawei-affiliated fund.
- At growth stage, "X for Y" titles read as seed-stage. Your own category name, **"forward-deployed growth"**, is stronger and you own it.

**Fix:** move Palantir into the "pattern" slide as one of several analogies. Suggested cover lines:
- "TripleDart + Slate: the AI-native growth company. We sign up to the pipeline number."
- "Forward-deployed growth: operators and AI agents that own the pipeline number."

## 7. What a modern growth-stage deck looks like (2026)

At $6.5M you're technically at Series A/B scale. You're not "late-stage" in VC terms, since most late-stage investors look for $30M+ ARR. The right frame is **"first institutional capital into a profitable, scaled business"**, which is a compelling story in its own right. The best growth decks in this window share these traits:

1. **Two artifacts, not one.** A *send-ahead* deck (15–18 slides, readable without you, memo-like) and a *presenting* deck (10–12 slides, visual, for the room). Plus a 25–35 slide appendix and a data room ready on day 1.
2. **Metrics first, story second.** Page 2 is the investment summary. Every claim has a number, and every number has a definition and a source.
3. **History plus plan on the same axes.** Actuals and forecast on one chart, with the forecast visibly anchored to the trend.
4. **Proof of AI leverage, not AI vocabulary.** In 2026 every deck says "agents". The ones that raise show revenue per FTE rising, gross margin expanding and cost-to-serve falling.
5. **Revenue-quality transparency.** Cohort charts are table stakes. Showing a weak metric with a credible fix beats hiding it.
6. **A live product.** A 60–90 second demo video or a sandbox login.
7. **Scenario thinking.** Base, upside and downside cases, plus a "what must be true" slide (you already have this).
8. **A partnership contract.** What you'll report (you already offer a monthly scorecard), what you want from the partner, and governance post-round.
9. **Brevity and hygiene.** No placeholders, consistent numbers across every page, one font system. Your design is already there.

## 12. Closing thesis

The October deck is a strong *category* deck. It's written for a seed or Series A investor who buys a vision. The investor you want, a growth partner who stays with you from $15M to $100M, buys three things: **(1) a trend they can extend, (2) revenue quality they can trust, and (3) a team and system that can absorb capital.** You have the raw material for all three: profitability, enterprise logos, an operating cadence and a growing product. The rewrite is about *showing your past and your proof*, telling the truth about retention and pricing (with the fix already in motion), and pointing the software story at evidence instead of analogies.

Do that, and "bootstrapped to $6.5M at ~50% EBITDA, now adding an AI product and an M&A engine" becomes one of the more interesting growth stories in this market. Investors will compete to partner with you rather than price you as an agency.
